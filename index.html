Project Overview

This project aims to develop a campus guide website designed specifically for freshmen, prospective students, and visiting guests. The platform helps users quickly become familiar with the campus, view building information, and find the correct routes before arriving or during their first days on campus.

At present, most universities only use traditional 2D maps. These maps can only show approximate locations, but they cannot clearly display what buildings look like, how their internal layouts are organized, or where entrances are located. This becomes especially problematic for connected teaching buildings such as SA, SB, SC, and SD, which have multiple entrances and complex structures. As a result, people often get lost, cannot find the correct entrance, and waste a significant amount of time.

To solve these problems, we upgraded the traditional 2D map by making the platform more intuitive, immersive, interactive, and user-friendly. The system is designed to help users understand the campus more quickly and comfortably while also promoting campus culture more effectively. In addition, it provides a practical new tool to support the development of a smart campus environment.

1. Motivation & Research

In the daily operation of universities and the adaptation process of new campus users, freshmen, prospective students, and visiting scholars usually rely on 2D flat maps provided by universities to understand the spatial layout of the campus. However, such traditional navigation methods have significant limitations in practical application.

Firstly, the dimension of information presentation is insufficient. Traditional 2D maps can only provide abstract geographical location relationships, lacking intuitive expression of building appearance, internal spatial structure, and functional layout, making it difficult for users to establish complete and accurate spatial cognition.

Secondly, the efficiency of practical use is relatively low. Some teaching buildings on campus (such as SA, SB, SC, SD, etc.) have multiple entrances and complex connected structures. Even when users arrive near the target building, they still struggle to locate the effective entrance quickly, resulting in problems such as getting lost and directional anxiety.

Thirdly, existing navigation modes lack interactivity and user experience. Users mostly receive information passively and lack willingness to participate and explore actively, making the campus navigation process monotonous and unable to meet the usage needs of young user groups.

Based on the above practical problems, this project proposes a core research question: how to help users perceive the campus space more intuitively and explore the campus more efficiently without increasing the usage threshold?

The Gap

Academic Papers

  • What They Did Well:
  • Many studies on virtual tours and gamification have shown:
  • 360° virtual tours can enhance users' sense of immersion and spatial cognition, allowing them to form an intuitive understanding of the real environment online [1].
  • Gamification mechanisms can effectively increase user engagement, exploration willingness, and platform activity, such as points, badges, and achievement systems that can enhance users' motivation to continue using [2].
  • Virtual tours can not only provide information but also enhance cultural transmission and community identity, especially suitable for educational and campus settings [3].
  • What They Missed:
  • Although existing research has verified the value of virtual tours and gamification, there are still some shortcomings:
  • Most studies focus more on "immersive experience" or "user engagement" and lack consideration of real campus usage scenarios, such as: difficulty in finding building entrances, and the difficulty in identifying multi-entry teaching buildings.
  • Some AR or VR systems in some studies are technically complex and have high requirements for device performance, which are not suitable for lightweight web platforms [4].
  • Research on gamification also points out that if the reward mechanism is overemphasized, users may only focus on "completing tasks" and ignore the true information acquisition and learning process [5].

Commercial Products

  • What They Did Well:
  • Existing commercial guide products (Google Maps, campus virtual tour websites, travel guide Apps, etc.) generally perform well in the following aspects:
  • Providing clear map structures and location markers.
  • Supporting image, text, or audio introductions.
  • Some virtual campus platforms offer 360° panoramic viewing, enhancing the visual experience.
  • Maturity in navigation functions, able to quickly help users find their destination.
  • What They Missed:
  • However, these commercial products still have obvious limitations:
  • Most products are more inclined towards "navigation tools," focusing on "how to get there," which is not suitable for the campus environment and does not meet the purpose of campus tour guides to "understand the campus."
  • Traditional map products lack: building background introductions, spatial immersion, and campus cultural experiences.
  • Very few products integrate: maps, 360° panoramic views, and game-like exploration mechanisms.

Stakeholders

Core Service Targets

Prospective And New Students

This group represents the primary users of the project. They are unfamiliar with the campus environment and therefore have strong needs for information access and spatial understanding.

  • They need to understand the distribution and functions of campus buildings in advance
  • They are more likely to get lost in complex buildings (e.g., teaching buildings with multiple entrances)
  • They have a high acceptance of immersive experiences (e.g., 360° panoramic views)
  • They are more likely to engage with check-in and achievement systems

Visiting Scholars And Guests

This group usually stays on campus for a short period and has limited prior knowledge of the environment, relying more on navigation tools to quickly obtain necessary information.

  • They focus on building purposes and locations
  • They require efficient and intuitive information presentation
  • They use the system less frequently, but their information needs are more concentrated

Secondary Service Targets

University Administrators

This group does not directly use the system, but they have an influence on its promotion and the accuracy of its content.

  • They are concerned with the presentation of the campus image
  • They help enhance the effectiveness of external publicity and promotion

Current Students (Partial On-Campus Users)

Although this group already has a certain level of familiarity with the campus, they may still get lost in complex buildings or in less frequently used areas.

  • They may need assistance in locating entrances or classrooms
  • They have both functional and entertainment-related needs

3. Ideation & Alternatives

The "Crazy Eights"

Design Alternatives

Option 1: AR Augmented Reality Tour

Identify buildings through mobile phone cameras, overlay historical information and virtual elements.

Option 2: Traditional map navigation+audio explanation

GPS based map navigation, accompanied by pre recorded audio explanations.

Option 3: Gamified Interactive Tour

Design the campus tour as a treasure hunt game, unlocking historical knowledge by completing tasks.


Reason for selection： The final choice for this project is Option Three: Gamified Interactive Tour. The key considerations were as follows:

Technical Feasibility
Compared to the AR technology in Option One, the gamified tour didn't need complex real-time image recognition, high computing power, or accurate positioning support, so it was much easier to develop. Basic web map functions, task-triggering logic, and QR code scanning were all that was required. This approach fitted the project's timeline and resources better, allowing for quicker development, testing, and deployment.

User Experience and Alignment with Project Goals

  • Increased User Engagement: Turning the passive campus tour into an active treasure-hunt style experience made it far more fun and immersive than static maps or voice-only guides.
  • Meeting Core Needs: Features like check-in tasks, an achievement system, and social leaderboards directly addressed new students' desire to "make friends while exploring," helping them expand their social circles on campus through interaction.
  • Enhanced Platform Reusability: The task unlocking and achievement collection mechanisms effectively encouraged users to revisit the platform and explore different campus areas, which significantly boosted long-term activity.
Integration of Advantages from Other Options
Even though Option One and Option Two weren't chosen as the core solution, their main strengths were still absorbed and included in the final design:
  • Borrowing the spatial information visualization idea from Option One, we added a one-click toggle between 2D and 3D maps for the main campus buildings. This balanced the clarity of traditional navigation with a more immersive feel.
  • Taking the positioning and navigation logic from Option Two, we implemented accurate user location functionality, so users always received clear directional guidance throughout the tour.
Comparison and Advantages of the Options
  • Option One (AR Tour): Although it looked impressive, it had poor device compatibility, high development costs, and major technical challenges, which could lead to a poor experience for some users.
  • Option Two (Traditional Map + Voice Guide): It was technically mature, but turned out to be boring and lacked interaction, so it didn't meet the project's main goals of increasing fun and social engagement.
Conclusion
Based on the above factors, the Gamified Interactive Tour achieved the best balance between technical feasibility, user experience, and project goals, making it the most suitable choice for this project.

Final Reflection

Social/Ethical Impact

This project is not only a functional campus navigation tool but also has certain social and ethical impacts, primarily reflected in campus culture dissemination, community connection, and privacy protection.

Promoting the Preservation of Campus Culture (Cultural Preservation)
By integrating building introductions, historical background, and visual content into the map, this project systematically presents information that was previously scattered, allowing users to gain a more comprehensive understanding of campus culture. For new students, prospective students, and visiting scholars, this "visual + interactive" navigation approach helps to:

  • Quickly understand the campus spatial layout and historical context
  • Build a sense of identity and belonging toward the campus in advance
  • Reduce feelings of unfamiliarity and alienation when first arriving on campus
Therefore, this project plays a role in digitally preserving campus culture.

Enhancing Community Cohesion (Community Engagement)
By introducing check-in and achievement mechanisms, the project transforms campus exploration from "passively receiving information" to "actively participating in an experience," thereby:
  • Encouraging students to leave their dormitories and explore different areas of the campus
  • Promoting interaction among students (e.g., sharing check-in progress, recommending locations)
  • Helping users quickly make new friends and easily adapt to campus life
  • Enhancing users' sense of participation and connection to the campus space
This gamified design not only increases fun but also subtly strengthens the overall cohesion of the campus community.

Privacy and Data Ethics Considerations (Privacy & Ethical Considerations)
While providing an immersive experience, this project also needs to address potential privacy concerns:
  • Image capture issues: When capturing panoramic images and building photos, efforts should be made to avoid capturing identifiable personal information (such as faces, license plates, etc.)
  • User data issues: During interviews, the personal wishes of respondents will be fully respected, and they will decide whether to disclose their real names in the portfolio research results.
  • Permissions and informed consent: When using features such as taking photos, user authorization should be obtained, and a clear privacy statement should be provided.

Trae AI and Figma were used for web development. Claude Opus 4.6 and Qwen3 Max Thinking were used for HTML coding and prompt engineering. DeepSeek V3 and V4 were used for copywriting optimization and translation. Google's Nano Banan Pro and Nano Banana 2 were used for sketching, project planning diagrams, and most importantly, panoramic image processing.

2. Development Phase & AI Application
Trae AI was used for prototyping, core development, type definitions, 360° view integration, map refactoring, QR scanning, and debugging. Claude Opus 4.6 and Qwen3 Max Thinking were primarily used for prompt crafting and HTML code writing. DeepSeek V3 and V4 handled copywriting optimization and translation. Google Nano Banan Pro and Nano Banana 2 were used to generate sketches, project planning diagrams, and critically, to process panoramic images. Deployment to Vercel was manual.

3. AI Contribution Summary
Trae AI generated most frontend components and feature logic. Claude Opus 4.6 and Qwen3 Max Thinking focused on prompt writing, code generation, and project analysis. DeepSeek models handled copy optimization and translation. Google Nano Banan tools produced visual planning materials including sketches and diagrams, with a key role in panoramic image processing.

4. Human Oversight & Quality Control
All AI-generated code and prompts were manually reviewed. Testing was performed manually on Android and iOS.

5. Limitations & Workarounds
AI poorly recognized image-based location data, solved by a human-defined Location type system. iOS gyroscope permission issues were resolved with manually added code.

6. Ethical & Practical Notes
No confidential data was shared with AI tools. AI served solely as a productivity aid. All third-party libraries and AI-generated outputs were vetted by the developer.
